The rules

How a race works

Two teams, one library of hands-on boards, and a Grandmaster on each side deciding who plays what. Here is every rule, game by game.

Host a match Try the games solo

The shape of a race

Two teams race to clear 10 levels. Each team has a Grandmaster who watches from a dashboard and assigns every teammate a role and their own game. No two teammates play the same one.

  1. Solve your game. Everyone plays their own assigned game, level by level. Clearing your board marks you cleared and earns your team currency.
  2. Wait or bonus. Cleared early? Hold your status for 180s while teammates finish, or gamble it on a harder bonus board for extra currency. Fail the bonus and you forfeit its loot and must re-clear.
  3. Advance together. The moment the whole team is cleared, everyone jumps to the next level.
  4. Grandmaster plays the meta. The Grandmaster spends the team's currency on perks: freeze or scramble an opponent, shield the squad, or extend a teammate's wait timer.
  5. Win. First team to clear level 10 takes the race.

A wrong answer on your board never costs you anything except time. You just get a fresh one. Speed and steadiness win relays.

Game 1 REWIRE

Puzzle · spatial reasoning

What you see

A 4×4 grid of wire tiles (straights, elbows, T-junctions and endpoints) scrambled into random rotations. One tile glows: the source. Two tiles are sinks.

How to play

  • Tap a tile to rotate it 90° clockwise. Tiles never move, only turn.
  • Powered wires light up live as you work, so you can see the current spread.
  • You win when power reaches every sink and no live wire dangles into a wall or a closed tile.

Main: 4×4 grid, 2 sinks · Holding: 2×2 grid, 1 sink

Trace a path backwards from each sink to the source first, then rotate along the route.

Game 2 SWEEP

Logical · deduction

What you see

A 6×6 minesweeper board hiding 6 mines, with a safe opening region already revealed. Every number counts the mines in its eight neighbours.

How to play

  • Tap a cell you've deduced is safe to reveal it.
  • Right-click (or long-press on touch) a cell you've deduced is a mine to flag it.
  • Flag all 6 mines, then submit. Revealing a mine ends the board instantly and you get a fresh one.

Main: 6×6, 6 mines · Holding: 3×3, 1 mine

Every board is solvable with pure logic, so no guessing is ever required. If you're guessing, there's a deduction you haven't spotted.

Game 3 MIRROR RUN

Divided attention · mazes

What you see

Two small mazes side by side, each with a runner and a flagged exit. A badge between them tells you how Runner B twists your controls: mirrored, rotated, or fully inverted.

How to play

  • Every move drives both runners at once: Runner A obeys you directly, Runner B follows the twisted version.
  • Swipe on the boards, tap the arrow pad, or use arrow keys / WASD.
  • A runner blocked by a wall stays put while the other still moves. That's the trick for splitting them up.
  • You win the moment both runners stand on their own flags after the same move.

Main: two 6×6 mazes, ~10–18 moves · Holding: two 4×4 mazes, ~3–6 moves

Park one runner against a wall that blocks it, then move the other freely. Walls aren't obstacles, they're parking brakes.

Game 4 DECANT

Sorting · planning

What you see

Six tubes holding stacks of four colours (plus empties), each tube holding up to four segments.

How to play

  • Tap a source tube, then a destination tube, to pour.
  • A pour moves one block, the top of the source tube, and it can go into any tube with room, even onto a different colour.
  • You win when every tube is empty or completely one colour. You can't get stuck, but sloppy pours cost precious seconds, and Undo is there if you need it.

Main: 6 tubes, 4 colours, capacity 4 · Holding: 3 tubes, 2 colours (~2 pours)

Keep an empty tube free as workspace as long as you can. Burying a colour under two others is what costs you.

Game 5 ECHO

Reflex / memory

What you see

A 3×3 grid of pads. They flash one at a time in a sequence of five, and the sequence plays only once.

How to play

  • Watch the whole sequence. Don't tap while it plays.
  • Then tap the pads back in exactly the same order.
  • One wrong tap fails the attempt and you get a brand-new sequence, so there's no second viewing.

Main: 3×3 pads, sequence of 5 · Holding: 2×2 pads, sequence of 3

Chunk it: remember "corner, centre, top…" as a shape traced across the grid, not as nine separate positions.

Game 6 OVERPRINT

Spatial · composition

What you see

A target pattern next to a workspace holding three transparent stamp layers. All layers print onto the same sheet, and the combined marks must match the target exactly.

How to play

  • Pick a layer chip, then slide it with the arrow pad (or tap a cell to drop it there).
  • ⟳ rotates a layer a quarter-turn; ⇄ flips the one layer that allows it.
  • Press CHECK when your print matches. Extra marks fail just like missing ones.

Main: 6×6 sheet, 3 layers · Holding: 4×4 sheet, 2 layers (slides, one turn)

Place the biggest stamp first, then fit the small ones into what's left of the target.

Game 7 STACKDROP

Causal prediction · planning

What you see

A drop chamber: balls resting on numbered pins, walls and ramps below them, and a container at the bottom for each shape. Nothing moves until you pull a pin.

How to play

  • Tap a pin to arm it, tap again (or press PULL) to remove it, then gravity resolves before your next pull.
  • ═ pins hold a ball. ╱ ╲ pins and ◣ ◢ ramps roll it one cell down-slope, so a slanted pin steers a ball while it's there and lets it drop straight through once it's gone.
  • Every ball must land in the container with its own shape. A hazard (✖) or the wrong container loses it.
  • A pulled pin never comes back, so the order is the puzzle. Undo doesn't exist; RESTART resets the whole chamber.

Main: 8×6 chamber, 2 balls, 4 pins (~3 pulls) · Holding: 5×4 chamber, 1 ball, 2 pins

Trace each ball's fall before you touch anything, then ask which slanted pins have to still be there when that ball goes past.

Game 8 LANE SHIFT

Scheduling · planning under pressure

What you see

A conveyor of lanes carrying shaped packets to the right. Each lane ends in an exit marked with a shape, and junctions (→ ↘ ↗) sit on the belt deciding where a packet goes as it leaves that cell.

How to play

  • Every turn you take exactly one action: flip a junction, spend a hold pad (⏸) to keep one packet still, or PASS. Then every packet advances one cell.
  • Each packet must reach the exit carrying its own shape.
  • Two packets landing on the same cell, a blocker (✖), the wrong exit, or falling off the top or bottom all lose the run.
  • The small arrow on a packet shows only its next step, so there's no further preview. No undo; RESTART resets the belt.

Main: 3 lanes × 6 columns, 3 packets, 2 junctions (~8 turns) · Holding: 2 lanes × 3 columns, 2 packets, 1 junction

The belt never waits for you, so plan backwards: work out which turn each packet reaches a junction, and flip it on the turn before.

Game 9 SHADOW CAST

Spatial reasoning · 3D orientation

What you see

A solid block built from a handful of cubes, drawn in 3D. Beside it are two target silhouettes: FRONT (the shadow it casts straight ahead) and TOP (the shadow it casts straight down), each with a live copy showing the shadows your block is casting right now.

How to play

  • Six buttons turn the block a quarter turn at a time around the X, Y or Z axis, one button each way. On a keyboard: x, y, z, with Shift for the other direction.
  • You win the moment both live shadows match their targets exactly.
  • A shadow cell fills if any cube sits behind it, so a shadow can't tell you how deep the block is, and several different poses can be correct.
  • Cells that don't match yet are ringed and marked ✕. Turning is free and reversible; RESTART puts the block back where it started.

Main: 6 cubes in a 4×4×4 box, 2 turns from a solution · Holding: 4 cubes in a 3×3×3 box, 1 turn

Read the two targets before you touch anything. Both are the same width, so that's how wide the block must sit; the FRONT target's height is how tall it must stand, and the TOP target's height is how far back it must reach. That usually rules out most of the six turns before you try one.

Game 10 THREADLINE

Routing · planning under constraints

What you see

A grid with a START socket, an END socket, numbered anchors, and blocked cells (✕). Above the board: which anchor you need next, how many bends you have spent, and how long your cable has run.

How to play

  • Draw one cable from START to END. Tap a neighbouring cell to extend it, drag across the grid to trace, or use the arrow keys.
  • The anchors must be reached in their numbered order. Stepping onto a later one early fails the run, it isn't ignored.
  • The cable can't enter a blocked cell or cross or touch itself; every cell is used at most once, so doubling back on your own line is out.
  • Every corner spends one bend, and the board only gives you so many. A red bar on an anchor is a port: the cable has to cross that particular side.
  • Tap a cell your cable already covers to rewind to it, or UNDO one step at a time. The board submits itself the moment a legal cable reaches END.

Main: 8×8, 3–5 anchors, 5–10 blocked cells, 6–10 bends · Holding: 5×5, one anchor, one obstacle

Bends, not length, are the scarce thing. Plan the corners first: count the turns a route needs before you draw a single cell, and prefer the one that lines an anchor up with the next one.

Game 11 BOMB DEFUSE

Manual lookup · divided attention under a fuse

What you see

A bomb: a big red countdown, a green OK button, a blue Give up, and six bays, some shut behind orange shutters, the live ones waiting to be worked. Plus a MANUAL button, because nothing on the bomb tells you what any of it means.

How to play

  • Two people play this. You are the Defuser; your Grandmaster has the manual on their dashboard. Describe the bay, they read you the rule. You have a copy too, but flipping to it hides the bomb while the fuse burns, so asking is faster than looking.
  • Tap a bay to open it. Maze: walk the blue marker to the red exit; the green tip tells you which of the manual's eight mazes you are in, and only the manual shows the walls. Simon Says: press the colour opposite the one that flashed, never the one you saw. According to number: find the displayed number in the grid the green 1 names, and press its column. The mini button: arm it, wait, then catch the red the instant it appears, hold until green, read the code and let go.
  • There are no strikes. One wrong move and the bomb goes off: a step into a wall, a wrong colour, a wrong button, OK pressed too early, or the fuse running out. A fresh bomb arrives five seconds later.
  • Press OK only once every live bay has shut behind its shutter. It starts flashing when they have, but on a bonus board that OK arms the next bank rather than ending it.

Main: 1 bay at level 1, 2 from level 3, 3 from level 6 · 90s fuse at level 1, tightening inside each band · Bonus boards come in banks: shut the first and a second arms behind it on a shorter fuse · Holding: one small bay, 35s

New to it? Practice mode has a ladder of fixed missions: one drill per bay, then the gauntlet.

Read the manual page before you start a bay, not during it. The Simon mapping is four lines and it never changes. Learn it once and you never open that page again, which is most of the fuse saved.

Duelist RPS DUEL

Head-to-head · nerve

The role

Your Grandmaster can spend one of the team's four playing slots on a Duelist, a champion who never solves a puzzle. Instead you fight the other team's Duelist, over and over, for the whole match. Both teams must field one, or neither does: a duel needs two seats. You don't pick your game either: the server does, from the four below.

Every round of every duel runs on a clock, shown on your duel card and counting down in red at the end. Each game below has its own natural window, but the host can set one timer for all of them in the lobby, from 3 seconds to 30 — so a group that wants faster duels just says so once.

How to play

  • A duel opens at kickoff and again 30 seconds after the last one ends. Pick rock, paper or scissors inside a 5-second window.
  • Neither of you sees the other's hand until the round resolves, not even your Grandmaster, so there's nobody to ask. Let the window lapse and you forfeit that round, so a guess always beats a freeze.
  • First to two takes the duel. Ties replay.

What it's worth

  • Win and you're your team's green light: they can't advance a level without you, and they can while you hold it. Your team banks 2 coins, doubling every duel you win in a row, up to 8.
  • Lose and your team is stuck until you win the next one, plus a 60-second lock that holds even after you win it back. That lock only lands once per level, so a bad run costs you time, not the match.

There's no system to solve here, only the person opposite you. Watch what they threw last round, and remember they're watching you.

Duelist CROWN DUEL

Head-to-head · counters and memory

How to play

  • You both hold five cards: King, Knight, Guard, Assassin, Peasant. Each round you both commit one inside a 10-second window. Only the same card against itself draws; every other pairing has a winner, who takes a Crown. Both cards are spent either way.
  • King beats the three fighters. Peasant beats King. All three fighters beat Peasant. Between them: Guard beats Knight, Knight beats Assassin, Assassin beats Guard.
  • Three rounds, most Crowns wins, and two of them settles it early. Level after the third and you both get fresh hands for Sudden Death, where the first round that isn't a draw takes the duel.

Royal Sacrifice

  • Once per duel, before any round, you can destroy two of your unused cards to rewrite a third into a different character. Never into a King.
  • Both of you commit to that decision secretly, and then it is announced: your opponent is told a sacrifice happened. They are never told which cards burned, which one changed, or what it became.
  • It costs you two cards out of the three rounds you have to play, so it buys surprise with tempo. Two Peasants and a King is a real hand — and nothing about it is guessable from the outside.

The card you played is public the moment the round resolves, so a duel is also a memory game: count what they have spent, and remember they are counting yours.

Duelist NUMBER CLASH

Head-to-head · spending

How to play

  • You both hold 1 to 9, each playable once. Every round you both commit one inside an 8-second window; the higher number takes a point, equal numbers draw, and both numbers are spent either way.
  • First to four points takes the duel, over at most seven rounds. Still level after that and it runs on until someone gets there.
  • Everything played is face up afterwards, so you always know exactly what is left in both hands.

Beating their 1 with your 9 wins the round and hands them the better trade. The hard question is never which number wins — it is which one is worth spending.

Duelist BID WAR

Head-to-head · bluff and economy

How to play

  • You both start with 20 coins. Five prizes worth 1 to 5 Victory Points go up one at a time, in an order the server shuffles. You can see the lot on the block and the one after it, never further.
  • Each round you both name an exact bid inside a 10-second window. The higher bid takes the prize — and both bids are spent. The losing one buys nothing.
  • Equal bids pay nobody and roll that prize into the next lot, so a mirror bid makes the following one bigger for both of you.
  • Most Victory Points takes the duel, however they were bought: three cheap lots lose to one 5. Coins left at the end are worth nothing. If the last lot ties, you both get a fresh five coins and bid again for it.

Twenty coins over five lots is four a lot on average. Everything you spend early is a lot you cannot contest later, and they are doing the same arithmetic about you.

Fair play, by design

Every player gets a different, randomly generated board, so your teammate's answer is useless to you and there's nothing to Google. The server re-checks every move you submit, so the only way through is to actually play.
